# ArchitectHire — Professional Tools

*Product roadmap and landing page reference. August 2026.*

---

## The frame

These aren't ten features. They're **one business development pipeline** — the function small practices are worst at, and the reason a professional stays subscribed in a month with no matches.

```
Profile → Intake → Booking → Proposal → Pipeline → Client updates
  ↑                                          ↑
  SEO & sharing                    Jurisdiction data feeds both
```

Leads are the acquisition hook. **Tools are the retention floor.**

The organising principle for what makes the cut: *does this only work because we run the matching platform and own the jurisdiction database?* If a generic tool could do it, it's low priority. If nobody else could build it, it goes first.

## Phase 1 — Ship now

Cheap to build, compounds immediately, safe to tease.

### Profile / mini-site

A public, indexed page per professional: portfolio, disciplines, licensure, jurisdictions worked, rates, contact.

**Why it's first.** It's our asset more than theirs.

- **SEO surface.** Thousands of legitimate pages targeting *architect Oakland CA*, *ADU designer Sacramento* — on a site whose entire strategy is organic.
- **Growth loop.** Most of these people have no website worth the name. Give them a clean URL and it goes in their email signature, Instagram bio, and business card. Every share is a backlink and a brand impression. Supply markets us for free.
- **Safe because of our model.** Public contact details would be suicidal on commission — owner finds architect, goes direct, we get nothing. On subscription they've already paid, so an off-platform meeting costs us nothing. This is the first real dividend of the pricing decision.

**Guardrail.** A profile with no images and two lines of bio reflects on us, not them. Set a minimum completeness bar before it goes public and indexed.

**Consider:** free claimable profiles for non-subscribers. The Houzz/Yelp playbook — get listed, get indexed, upsell into matches. Fills the directory before demand arrives, costs nothing.

### Instant reply

An auto-response the moment a match lands — in their voice, acknowledging the project, offering times.

**Why it matters more than it looks.** Our single worst supply problem is architects who take three days to answer. This isn't a feature so much as a patch on a behavioural flaw that would otherwise cost us owner trust and, eventually, the 48-hour promise.

### Fee schedule builder

Rates, phases, and add-ons stored once, reused everywhere.

Boring, but it's the required input for proposals — so it's not wasted work, it's sequencing.

### Pipeline view

Active pursuits on a simple board. We already have the data; this is presentation, not engineering.

Also does quiet work on the pursuit-slot mechanic — professionals can see what's clogging their cap.

## Phase 2 — The differentiator

### Jurisdiction lookup ★

Query access to our 3,200-jurisdiction database: submittal requirements, typical plan check timelines, local amendments, common rejection reasons.

**This is the one to push hardest.**

- Nobody else can build it. Every other tool here has a generic competitor; this one has none.
- It makes our core asset serve both sides of the market instead of just powering estimates.
- An architect taking work in an unfamiliar city would subscribe for this alone.
- It reinforces the brand position directly — we're the authority on how building actually works (Brand Wiki §3).

**Obligation.** Once professionals rely on this, wrong data is a professional liability issue, not a bad search result. Jurisdictions change rules constantly. This makes database maintenance an operating function, not a content project.

### AI intake assistant

**Not a generic chatbot.** Every website builder ships one and it mostly annoys people.

The strong version is narrow: **it qualifies before it books.** It knows the project type from the owner's questionnaire, asks the three or four things the architect always has to ask anyway — existing drawings? permitted work? timeline? budget reality? — and hands over a briefed conversation instead of a cold calendar invite.

That's pre-qualification, not chat. Worth real money to someone whose scarcest resource is unbilled hours.

### Booking

Self-serve scheduling for consults, plan reviews, and scoping calls. Shortens the path from "saw this profile" to "on a call."

**The hidden reason to build it:** a booked consult is an **observable event.** We lost outcome visibility when we dropped escrow. Booking recovers the first conversion step — match → conversation — without touching money. Cheapest instrumentation we'll ever build, and the metric we need to sell subscriptions credibly.

### Scope templates

Standard scope by project type — ADU, addition, change of use, TI. Defines what a permit set actually includes so proposals stop underscoping.

Prerequisite for the proposal engine.

## Phase 3 — The moat

### Proposal generation

Every proposal tool on the market starts from a blank page. Ours starts from a **structured brief, a jurisdiction complexity score, and a pricing range** — because the owner already answered the questionnaire.

Nobody else can pre-populate scope, fee range, and likely permit path on day one. This is the feature that makes the subscription justify itself in a dry month, and it's the most defensible thing on this list after jurisdiction lookup.

**Two non-negotiable constraints:**

1. **The professional owns the output.** A draft they edit and send. Never auto-send, never presented as ArchitectHire's number. We just exited liability for delivery — we don't walk back in through the proposal.
2. **Underscoping is the failure mode.** If a generated draft misses required structural scope and the architect sends it, they eat the difference and blame us. Generate conservatively, flag uncertainty, require scope confirmation before render.

### Client updates

A shared project timeline the owner can see. Reduces "where are we?" emails, which architects universally hate.

Also worth noting: this is the one tool that touches the owner side post-match, so it needs a careful read against "we introduce, we don't intermediate."

## What we don't build

**CAD or drawing tools.** Competing with Autodesk on their turf, badly.

**Invoicing or payments.** We deliberately exited the money layer. Invoicing drags us back toward looking like a party to the transaction and reintroduces the liability we just shed.

**Generic CRM.** If it isn't powered by the brief or the jurisdiction data, it's a commodity and we lose to purpose-built tools.

## The thing to watch

**Tools retain professionals who are getting matched. They don't rescue professionals who aren't.**

If Oakland produces eight projects a month, no proposal generator fixes that churn — and building one instead of building demand is the most expensive mistake available right now.

This is why the sequence is what it is. **Ship the profile because it creates demand.** Hold the proposal engine until density justifies it.
