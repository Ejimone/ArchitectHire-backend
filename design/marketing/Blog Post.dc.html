Hiring an architect used to mean a stack of in-person meetings before you even knew a price. It doesn't anymore. Here's how to vet licensure, scope your project, and pay safely — entirely remotely — and still walk away with a stamped, permit-ready set.

1. Confirm the license before anything else

A drawing set is only worth submitting if it's stamped by an architect licensed in the state where you're building. That's not a formality — an unlicensed set will be rejected at plan check, no matter how good the design looks.

Ask for the license number and the issuing state, then verify it against the state board's public registry. On our platform this is done for you: every match is verified as actively licensed in your state before it ever reaches you.

2. Scope the project in writing

Before pricing means anything, both sides need to agree on what's being drawn. A tight scope prevents the two most common blow-ups: surprise costs and missed drawings at permit time.

A complete residential scope usually covers:

  • Existing conditions (as-built plans or a recent survey)
  • Proposed floor plans, elevations, and a site plan
  • Structural coordination and any required calculations
  • Energy-code compliance for your jurisdiction
  • A permit-ready, stamped set as the deliverable

3. Get a real number up front

"It depends" is not a quote. Modern platforms price a drawing set from square footage, add-ons, and a jurisdiction complexity score, giving you an itemized range before you talk to anyone. Use that as your baseline, then let each architect confirm a fixed quote or hourly rate on the scoping call.

4. Pay through escrow, not up front

Remote work only feels safe when the money does too. Escrow holds your funds and releases them as you approve milestones — so you're never paying for work you haven't seen. Avoid any arrangement that asks for the full fee before drawings begin.

"The best remote projects feel boring — clear scope, a real number, milestones that release on approval. Boring is what gets you a clean permit."

— Maya E., AIA · Licensed CA

5. Keep a tracked timeline to permit

Once drawings are underway, everything should live in one place: files, comments, milestone approvals, and the submission itself. A tracked timeline keeps revision cycles short and gives your contractor a set they recognize — which is what clears plan check the first time.

Do these five things and remote hiring isn't a compromise — it's faster, cheaper, and just as accountable as walking into a firm's office.
